A stage is a cube. Three walls and a floor the players can see; a fourth, invisible, facing the house; a ceiling nobody looks at. For a century the whole of theatre's self-awareness has consisted of turning around and addressing that fourth wall — and by now it has been done so often that the gesture has no force left in it. The wink to camera is not a rupture. It is the house style of a culture that needs its consumers reassured, all day, that they are consumers.

We count differently. We count from the inside out, beginning before there is any room at all, and we keep counting past the ceiling. A wall, for us, is not a surface. It is a threshold a story has to cross on its way from one person's interior to the common world. Each threshold costs something. Each returns something. The order cannot be skipped.

Here they are.

## I · The Spark

<p class="gloss">a point</p>

Before the word. The first animating idea: the urge to make, the vision that arrives unbidden and will not leave. It is sincere or it is nothing. A world can be built on borrowed sparks — on other people's visions, on forms already proven — and such worlds are built constantly, and you can feel the difference in the room within a minute.

The Gnostics told it as Sophia. Being the whole, she had nothing outside herself by which to know herself; and out of that longing she looked at her own reflection, and the one became two, and a universe fell out of the look. Every production that has ever mattered began the same way — as a wholeness that wanted, for no reason anyone can give, to see itself.

Geometrically it is a point. It has position and no extent. It cannot yet be shared.

## II · The Word

<p class="gloss">a line</p>

The spark speaks. What was a pressure becomes a dialogue with itself, and the dialogue becomes text: a script, a score, a storyboard, a liturgy. The point becomes a line.

This is the first sacrifice and the first gift. The word is flatter than the vision — it has to be, to travel — and in exchange it can be carried by people who were never there and read by people not yet born. A script is an invocation that has agreed to hold still. It is also, precisely, what you are calling in. Write carelessly and you will be answered carelessly.

## III · Where and When

<p class="gloss">the floor and the two side walls</p>

The line stands up into space and time. A room, a date, a duration nobody can pause. The booking; the rehearsal that drives the words into bodies; the design; the light. This is the unglamorous wall — the integrity of getting the text well-lit and well-held — and it is the one most often skimped, because it looks like logistics.

It is not logistics. It is the drawing of the circle. Inside the circle, different rules apply, and everyone present has agreed to them. Foundation, space, time: the stage proper.

## IV · The Front Wall

<p class="gloss">the proscenium — the players reach the house</p>

The wall that faces the house. In the old count it is the one you break by turning to the audience. In ours it is the entire relationship with them: who this is for, how they are found and drawn, what they are asked to give and what they are promised in return. Not a ticket. Attention — and beneath attention, an amount of energy we do not have a polite word for, offered on trust, to receive a circulation.

It is also where the metaphysics shows. The gap between the stage and the seats is the same gap as the one between this world and the other one. When ritual was denatured into theatre, the participants were seated in the dark and told to watch; when spirit was denatured into religion, the same thing happened to the same people. The fourth wall is the veil. To work on it at all is to work on the veil, whether or not anyone in the building says so.

Our players work across it deliberately — not by stepping off the stage, which is cheap, but by doing energetic work on the room as part of the performance. Opening. Circulating. Returning.

## V · The Back Wall

<p class="gloss">the house reaches back</p>

The front wall is the players reaching the house. The back wall is the house reaching the players. Spectators step into the verb. Not all of them, and nobody is shamed for keeping a seat — but the door is open to everyone, and it is held so exquisitely that the least practised person in the room, speaking a poem into a microphone for the first time, sounds beautiful and knows it.

Authentic relating. Collective tone. A dance that breaks out in the middle of things. The point of each is the same: that people discover their own impulses have fire in them — that they too could have a feral first-wall vision and bring it into the world. Vibrating with people, instead of consuming other people's vibration.

Timing is everything at this wall. Open it too early and the trance collapses into a workshop. Too late and it is an afterthought. The break must arrive when the room has already become one organism.

## VI · The Ceiling

<p class="gloss">the play becomes reality</p>

The wall nobody looks at, through which everything eventually leaves. The sixth wall is crossed when an artifact of the play changes how people interact with reality — and, if it is crossed well, changes people who were never there. A practice shown once in a room alters how a business is run, or how strangers behave in a square, or what someone takes the good and the true and the beautiful to be, so that they start baking for their neighbours. A crossing of the hearth. In the extreme case, the world depicted in the production is now the world some people live in.

*Cult* is from *cultus* — a thing tended. Tended by enough people for long enough, a play world becomes the ground everyone else stands on. Money. Nation. Market. Faith. Every one of them began on a stage and won.

This is the wall we are named for, and the one we build toward from the first day.

## Past the ceiling

The six walls make a stage, and the stage makes a world. The walls that follow are not about the production any more. They are about what the production was for. We say less about them because we have passed through them less.

### VII · Self and Other

The sixth wall dissolves the distance between cult and culture. The seventh dissolves the distance between one person and another: the self felt, directly, in the other; the verbs that make up *I* seen beautifully expressed in a stranger. *Love your neighbour as yourself* is the seventh wall, said plainly. It takes more trauma processed and more compassion than most of us have yet. The collective stories that get in the way of it are the ones the earlier walls exist to loosen.

### VIII · Being and Non-being

The barrier between self and no-self. The most disciplined traditions we know of spend lifetimes at this wall, and we have the decency not to pretend we have been further than its threshold.

### IX · The Sacrifice

Brahma is dismembered to make the world. At the ninth wall the transcendence of being and non-being sparks the germination of a new universe — or, in the small, a new idea. The ninth wall is the first. The child stretches the sac it is held in until the sac gives, and is born into a new reality, and at once begins to want.

So the count is a loop, and the loop is an octave.

## The casting principle

One rule follows from all of this, and we use it to cast.

You cannot open a wall for others that you have not passed through yourself — and in fact you must have passed through the one *beyond* it. To speak to an audience across the fourth wall you must have broken the fifth: crossed, at some point in your life, from the house to the stage, or you are only reciting. To bring an audience through the fifth, onto ground as uncertain as a stage, you must have broken the sixth: the play must be real for you. And to inspire people to break the sixth — to carry a world into their own lives — you must have broken the seventh. You must love them as yourself, and see their enchantment before they do.

Most good actors have broken the fifth wall and not the sixth. They feel the lines; they do not live in the world. A production needs a few people who do. Without them the magic does not outlast the curtain. With them it does, and nobody can quite say why.

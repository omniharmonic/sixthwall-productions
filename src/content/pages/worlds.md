An immersive world is not a show with the seats removed. Any six-walled production is immersive by default; it implicates the audience or it is not six-walled. A world is something else. It persists past the night. It has a cosmology, a cast, a liturgy, a lexicon, a gesture, a hymn — enough structure that it can be entered by people who were not at the premiere, and enough looseness that it keeps recreating itself without its makers in the room. At some point it is no longer clear where the production stops and the city begins. That is the point.

Here are the ones we have built, and what each taught us.

## The Herd of God

*Have you heard of God?*

A neo-kabbalistic doomsday church that worships humanity's first god, the Calf — whose High Priest, a former Wall Street trader, was struck blind before the Charging Bull one morning and found out. Eight sleeping angels, each replaced in our age by an adequate counterfeit. A god at the crown of the tree, a husk kept alive by the worship of people who love it strictly for what it yields. Thirty delegates of humanity. A ritual of ascent, attempted three times. The third, *Burning Angel, Perfect Mind*, was performed on the eighth of August, 2026.

The Herd is our clearest sixth wall. It began as a one-man revival show, became a cell, became a congregation; it now has a theology in four pamphlets, a creed, a sign made with the hands, hymns that people hum without meaning to, and a growing number of participants who are no longer entirely sure which parts of it they believe. That uncertainty is not a failure of the work. It is the work. One night happened, and the world it opened has kept recreating itself ever since.

It is also where the rite is most visible. The form is a megachurch service, raised as straight as we could build it — the call and response, the testimony, the altar call, the glass of milk. The object at the centre of it is the one thing every child of the book has been told is the idol. Hold those side by side and the room has to ask what, exactly, it is enjoying, and why it feels so much like church. Then the calf burns, and the question is no longer theirs to answer from a seat.

## Independence, the Musical

Begins as the straightest musical we could write — overture, exposition, every character introduced one line at a time at the front of the stage and sent to the back — and then leaves the building. *We are going outside now, and when we leave, this frame is over.* There is an art car. The rules belong to a video game. A song every person in the room learned as a child turns out to be about colonisation, which it always was, and is sung that way for the first time.

We will keep making musicals and we will keep utterly destroying what a musical is, because *the musical* is the most perfectly hollowed vehicle in the language. Add it to the end of anything and people know what they are about to see. They do not. But they come in.

## The Creative Cauldron

The prototype. A variety night with no intermission, curated live from the energy of the room rather than from a running order — established forms first, singers and sacred dance and the group attuned into one organism, and then the forms deliberately broken by a trickster, in a way that handed everyone present permission to bring what they had. Poetry from people who had never read aloud. The intensity of feeling that a room can only hold once it has stopped being an audience.

It ran in New York, and once in Boulder, and the spell after each one lingered in the people who had been there for weeks. One of us left a career in finance to follow it. Its limit is what taught us the rest: without structure, the spell is a consumable experience, and it is gone within a month. The worlds above are what we built so that it would stay.

## What a world needs

Every one of these has the same bones, and we now build them in deliberately.

A **cosmology** — a claim about the nature of reality, held with enough conviction to call something down. A **cast** who have broken the sixth wall themselves, for whom the play is real, because an audience can tell. A **liturgy** — the repeatable forms that let a person who was not at the first night take part without being taught. A **lexicon**, because a world you cannot speak in is a world you cannot live in. A **gesture** and a **song**, because the body remembers what the mind lets go of. And a hole in it somewhere: a question the world itself cannot answer, left open on purpose, so that it keeps being worth returning to.

If the thing you are building has most of these, we would like to hear about it.
